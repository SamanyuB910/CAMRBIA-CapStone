\begin{abstract}
\noindent
R-Lens uses the J-Lens fitting pipeline but replaces ordinary-gradient transport
with LRP-derived relevance coefficients, applying modified backward rules for
normalisation and gated MLP components while leaving the forward pass unchanged.
Its authors report, qualitatively and without a released scorer, that R-Lens
readouts at early layers are more coherent than J-Lens readouts. We evaluate that claim on two 27B models with a blinded,
pre-specified and frozen rating panel of 200 three-arm cells, scored by two
independently validated LLM autoraters.

Under a standard prompt-aware coherence rubric, R-Lens is rated more coherent
than J-Lens on both models: $+0.690$, 95\%~CI \ci{0.520}{0.855} on Qwen3.5-27B
and $+1.010$, \ci{0.785}{1.235} on Gemma-3-27B-it, on a 0--4 scale. R-Lens also
surfaces significantly more prompt-overlapping content than J-Lens.

We therefore attempted, post hoc, to measure coherence with prompt-local content
excluded: a second rubric instructing judges to discard tokens recoverable from
the prompt and score only what remains. Under it the pooled R--J difference falls
from $+0.690$ to $+0.325$ \ci{0.165}{0.480} on Qwen and from $+1.010$ to $+0.110$
\ci{-0.045}{0.255} on Gemma.

\flag{That second instrument did not validate, and we report it as an exploratory
measurement attempt rather than a second endpoint.} Its inter-judge agreement is
slight ($\kappa = 0.19$ against $0.51$); on Gemma its two judges disagree in sign
with disjoint intervals; and in a mixture-invariance control holding a fixed
coherent core while varying only the ignorable prompt-local padding, one judge
held steady and the other's scores declined monotonically with padding in half of
all families. Excluding prompt-local content therefore did not produce a reliable
cross-judge measure. That is itself a result: the decomposition of ``coherence''
into prompt-local and non-prompt-local components is measurement-sensitive.

The contribution is therefore neither a confirmation nor a refutation. R-Lens is
rated more coherent under a standard rubric, robustly and on both models. It also
receives more credit for prompt-local content --- which includes orthographic
normalisation such as \emph{poeple}\,$\to$\,\emph{people}, plausibly genuine
computation rather than copying. A post-hoc, rule-selected cell shows the two rubrics ranking
the same three arms in opposite orders. The token lists themselves make the
construct distinction visible; the numerical reversal that accompanies them
still depends on the instrument. We also show
that restricting to cells with equal observed echo --- the natural first response
to this confound --- gives a markedly more reassuring answer than rubric-based
exclusion, because it conditions on a variable the lens itself affects. Neither
method cleanly identifies the contribution of prompt-local content.
\end{abstract}

\section{What this document does and does not claim}

This is an independent evaluation of the coherence claim, and a methodological
extension of it. It is \emph{not} an exact replication: the source post released
no scorer, so the rubric, panel and statistics here are ours, and the released
lens artifacts we evaluate were fit with a lighter recipe than the one the post
describes (\S2).

It asks two questions. First, when a rater is shown three unlabelled
early-layer readouts of the same prompt --- one from the logit lens, one from
J-Lens, one from R-Lens --- does the R-Lens arm receive a higher
contextual-coherence score? Second, does any advantage survive when the rater is
forbidden from crediting content that is \emph{prompt-local} --- direct
repetition together with trivial and normalised lexical variants?

It makes \textbf{no} claim about concept specificity, causal onset, ablation,
necessity, sufficiency, answer smuggling, or whether any lens recovers a
representation the model actually uses. Separate experiments in this project
address some of those; by pre-specification their results are not pooled with,
used to interpret, or reported alongside the coherence estimand, and they do
not appear here.

\paragraph{Terminology.} The protocol was written, frozen and hashed before any
rating was collected, but it was not deposited with a third-party registry. We
therefore say \emph{pre-specified and frozen} throughout, never
\emph{preregistered}.

\paragraph{\flag{The two stages were not specified at the same time.}} The
standard-rubric panel, rubric and analysis were frozen before any rating. The
non-echo analysis was \emph{not}: it was motivated after inspecting the
prompt-echo result from the standard rubric. Its rubric, salt, controls and
analysis procedure were then frozen and hashed before any non-echo rating was
collected, and its copy control was run and passed before the panel was scored.
It is therefore a \emph{staged methodological extension}, not part of the
original pre-specified endpoint, and readers should weight it accordingly. The
same applies to the small-sample analyses in \S\ref{sec:smallsample} and the
qualitative examples in \S\ref{sec:examples}, both of which are post-hoc.

\section{Artifacts and provenance}

Both lenses are the authors' released artifacts, not refits, taken at a pinned
commit. A preflight check refuses to run if either lens fails provenance:

\begin{itemize}
\item J-Lens carries \texttt{\{"estimator": "standard"\}}.
\item R-Lens carries \texttt{\{"estimator": "relp"\}} with the LN rule,
      identity rule and half rule at $\beta = 0.5$.
\item \flag{Both were fit with \texttt{n\_prompts: 25}}---the released light
      recipe, not the $n = 1000$ recipe described in the source post. Any
      difference between our effect size and theirs is confounded with this.
\item The two Jacobian sets are verified to actually differ, so a null could
      not be produced by silently loading the same lens twice.
\end{itemize}

Preflight additionally verifies hook indexing (that recorded block outputs
satisfy $\texttt{activations}[\ell]$ equals $\texttt{hidden\_states}[\ell{+}1]$),
target layer identity, and transport orientation. A failure at any of these is
fail-closed: the run aborts rather than proceeding with a warning.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Panel}

Twenty prompts---four from each of the five official evaluation sets
(association, multihop, multilingual, poetry, typo)---were selected by a hash
of a fixed salt, the set name and a stable item identifier, so selection is
reproducible and independent of any result. For the two sets carrying a target
(multihop, multilingual) only items the model itself answers correctly were
eligible, matching the source protocol's correctness filter.

\textbf{The eligible pool is the intersection}
$\mathcal{E}_{\text{Qwen}} \cap \mathcal{E}_{\text{Gemma}}$: an item is eligible
only if \emph{both} models answer it correctly, and the four prompts per set are
drawn from that shared pool by hash. Both models are therefore read on the same
20 items, and no item enters for one model on the strength of the other's
correctness alone.

Each prompt is read at five pre-specified relative depths
$z = \ell / \ell^{*} \in \{0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4\}$, giving
$20 \times 5 \times 2 = 200$ cells, each holding three arms. Readout position
follows the source protocol: the final prompt token, except poetry, read at the
newline ending line one.

\subsection{Blinding}

Arm order is permuted per \emph{(cell, rater)} pair, not once per cell. A single
fixed order would make position bias identical across judges and therefore
invisible to any agreement statistic. The unblinding key was written outside the
repository and outside the published result tree; the panel and the sample carry
content hashes; and every outgoing judge payload was reconstructed after the
fact and scanned for structural leakage of lens identity.

\subsection{Rubric}

Three dimensions per arm: \textbf{contextual coherence} (0--4, primary),
\textbf{lexical integrity} (0--2) and \textbf{prompt echo} (0--2). Contextual
coherence asks whether the readout tokens form a plausible continuation or
elaboration of the prompt's content. Prompt echo measures how much of the
readout is copied from the prompt --- it is scored so that copying can be
detected, not rewarded.

\subsection{Judges}

Two primary autoraters, \texttt{openai/gpt-5} and
\texttt{deepseek/deepseek-chat-v3.1}, each independently validated on a 78-cell
battery of control categories before being admitted. Validation gates include
position-bias rate (tested with an exact binomial, so that a threshold finer
than the achievable resolution at that sample size returns
\textsc{underpowered} rather than a false pass), a corruption-detection control,
a duplicate-arm control, and a response-completeness gate that fails a judge
returning empty content on more than 2\% of cells. One candidate judge was
rejected under exactly this last gate.

The primary score is the \textbf{mean of the two validated judges}. A third
judge (\texttt{meta-llama/\allowbreak{}llama-3.1-70b-instruct}) was originally used to
adjudicate the 63\% of cells on which the primaries disagreed, producing a
median-of-three score. \flag{That adjudicator did not clear validation}, and the
primary estimate was therefore demoted to the mean of two before any result was
written up. Section~\ref{sec:adjudicator} gives the gate it failed to resolve,
and Section~\ref{sec:judge} reports the adjudicated estimate as a sensitivity
analysis.

\paragraph{This change increases the reported effect} (pooled $+0.778 \to
+0.850$), and we state that explicitly because a reader is entitled to ask
whether the rule was chosen for its result. It was not: the demotion follows
from an instrument failing its admission gate, the gate was specified before the
adjudicator was run, and the direction and significance of every contrast are
unchanged either way. Both estimates appear in this document.

\subsection{Statistics}

The estimand is the equal-weight paired difference: mean over evaluation sets,
then prompts within set, then depths within prompt, so that no set or prompt is
over-weighted by having more cells. Intervals are percentile 95\% from a 10{,}000
replicate stratified paired \emph{prompt-cluster} bootstrap. $p$-values are
paired prompt-cluster sign-flip permutation tests --- never a bootstrap sign
proportion. Depth and set breakdowns are Holm-corrected within model.

\paragraph{What the intervals cover.} The bootstrap resamples \emph{prompts}, so
every interval here quantifies variation across prompts \emph{conditional on the
rubric and the two judges we used}. It does not represent uncertainty over the
population of possible evaluators. With two judges a random-effects model over
raters is not available, so judge disagreement is reported directly --- per-judge
estimates side by side --- rather than folded into a width.

Two features of this table matter as much as the headline.

\textbf{J-Lens is not distinguishable from the logit lens on either model}
($+0.045$, $p = 0.776$ on Qwen; $+0.430$, $p = 0.112$ on Gemma). On Qwen the two
are almost exactly equal in mean score, 1.295 against 1.250 on a 0--4 scale.
Under the primary rule the entire measured advantage over the logit-lens
baseline is carried by the R-Lens variant, not by the Jacobian transport as
such. Under the adjudicated rule the Qwen contrast is instead positive and
significant, and Section~\ref{sec:judge} shows it is judge-unstable in sign; it
should not be quoted as a single number in either direction.

On Gemma the bootstrap interval for J $-$ logit excludes zero (\ci{0.055}{0.790})
while the permutation test does not reject ($p = 0.112$). The two procedures ask
slightly different questions and the permutation test is the pre-specified one,
so we read that contrast as not significant --- but the disagreement is reported
rather than settled by taking whichever of the two is more favourable.

\textbf{Absolute scores are low.} Mean contextual coherence is 1.99 (Qwen) and
2.57 (Gemma) for R-Lens, against 1.30 and 1.56 for J-Lens and 1.25 and 1.13 for
the logit lens (Figure~\ref{fig:primary}a). On this rubric the mid-scale band is
``partially relevant, not a coherent continuation''. R-Lens readouts at
$z \le 0.4$ are better than the alternatives and still not good; on Qwen the
R-Lens mean does not reach the midpoint of the scale.

\flag{The depth profiles differ between models.} On Gemma the advantage holds at
every depth and survives Holm correction at all five: $+0.825$ at $z = 0$, a peak

models support is that R-Lens is ahead across $z \le 0.4$; a sharper claim about
\emph{where} within early depth the improvement sits is not supported by these
two models together.

\subsection{By evaluation set}

Per-set differences are all positive on both models and range from $+0.425$
(Qwen typo; Gemma multilingual) to $+2.150$ (Gemma poetry). \textbf{No per-set
difference survives Holm correction} ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.641$ throughout).
With four prompts per set this analysis is descriptive only and is reported for
pattern, not for inference. Poetry is the largest difference on both models,
but on four prompts that is an observation, not a finding.

\section{Robustness}

\subsection{Integrity audit}

A fail-closed audit re-derived the frozen artifacts from scratch: it recomputed
the panel hash, replayed the per-(cell, rater) arm permutation and compared it
to the key, reconstructed all 400 outgoing judge payloads and scanned them for
lens-identity leakage, and re-parsed every stored judge response.

Twenty conditions were checked. On the first pass nineteen held---including
zero unparseable responses across 526 stored ratings, zero arm-mapping
mismatches, and an exact reproduction of the panel hash
(\texttt{a4984e34\allowbreak{}d835c60e\allowbreak{}cb661552\allowbreak{}ec0a794e}
\dots).

One condition failed, and it was ours. The score-combination rule derived the
combined winner by \emph{voting} on the judges' winners while \emph{averaging}
their scores, which let the two disagree on 18 of 200 cells. The rule was
corrected to derive the winner from the combined scores, and the frozen raw
responses were re-combined without any new API call, so no cell was re-rated and
no rating changed.

\textbf{Re-running the full analysis on the corrected scores changed no reported
number}---all 18 cells were ties under both rules. The audit was then re-run
against the corrected ratings and the analysis those ratings produced, and
\textbf{all twenty conditions pass}, including
\texttt{winner\_matches\_max\_score} across all 200 cells and
\texttt{ratings\_frozen\_before\_analysis}. A defect that changes no number is
still reported here, because whether it changed a number is not something a
reader should have to take on trust.

\subsection{The adjudicator, and why it is not in the primary estimate}
\label{sec:adjudicator}

The original design adjudicated disagreements with a third judge. That judge was
put through the same 78-cell battery as the primaries and cleared eight of nine
gates outright: it never preferred a corrupted arm (0/10), showed no position
bias ($\chi^2 = 0.66$ on 2 df), scored provably-identical arms identically,
returned a tie on 5/5 identity-layer cells, showed full dynamic range on
late-layer controls, and left 0/190 responses unparseable.

It did not clear the order-invariance gate. Admission used the frozen
\emph{operational} rule --- an observed order-flip rate of at most 15\% --- not
an inferential non-inferiority test; GPT-5 passed at 1/15 (6.7\%) and the
adjudicator failed at 10/53. Intervals are reported below because both estimates
carry considerable uncertainty, and neither judge's true rate is established. Rotating the arm order flipped the winner on \textbf{10 of 53
non-tied pairs (18.9\%, 95\% CI \ci{9.4\%}{32.0\%})} against a 15\% ceiling.
An exact binomial test cannot reject 15\% ($p = 0.266$), and the interval is far
too wide to establish that the rate is acceptable. Escalating the battery does
not help: resolving 18.9\% against 15\% at 90\% power needs roughly 810
comparable pairs, a control panel several times the size of the experiment it
exists to validate. For scale, the admitted primary judge GPT-5 scored 1/15
(6.7\%) with an interval of \ci{0.2\%}{32\%}---so this control cannot even
separate the adjudicator from a judge already in use.

\flag{We therefore treated the unresolved gate as a failure and demoted the
adjudicator}, rather than admitting an instrument the battery could not clear or
loosening the threshold to fit it. We do \emph{not} claim to have shown the
adjudicator is order-biased: the correct statement is that it did not satisfy a
pre-specified validation gate, and the data cannot establish that its true rate
is either acceptable or unacceptable. The primary estimate is the mean of the
two validated judges. The adjudicated estimate is retained as a sensitivity
analysis, and the adjudicator's ratings are preserved rather than deleted, since
they are evidence about the panel even when not used to score it.

\paragraph{Disclosure of timing.} The battery and its 15\% ceiling were fixed in
the frozen protocol before any judge was run. The \emph{consequence} of
excluding the adjudicator, however, was already visible: the judge-dependence
sensitivity analysis (\S\ref{sec:judge}), which reports the mean-of-two estimate
at $+0.850$ against the adjudicated $+0.778$, was computed before the
adjudicator's battery was run. We therefore describe the demotion as a
transparent post-validation analysis amendment against a pre-specified gate,
not as a fully blind fail-closed decision. Both estimates appear throughout.

\subsection{Judge dependence}
\label{sec:judge}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig3_judge_sensitivity.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{R $-$ J under four scoring rules.} ``Mean of two'' is the
primary rule; ``Adjudicated'' is the original rule, retained as a sensitivity
analysis after its third judge failed to clear validation. The shaded band is a
diagnostic computed only on cells where the two primary judges disagreed; it is
conditioned on disagreement and is not a fifth estimate of the same quantity.}
\label{fig:judge}
\end{figure}

A two-judge mean is still a scoring rule, and a different rule could give a
different answer. Recomputing under four frozen rules---GPT-5 alone; DeepSeek
alone; the mean of the two (primary); and the original adjudicated rule---gives
a positive R $-$ J on every rule and on both models, with every
individual-judge interval excluding zero. The pooled estimate ranges from
$+0.725$ (DeepSeek alone) to $+0.975$ (GPT-5 alone), with the primary rule at
$+0.850$ and the adjudicated rule at $+0.778$.

The spread across rules (0.25 points pooled) is smaller than the effect itself,
and no rule places the interval anywhere near zero. \textbf{The adjudicated
estimate---the one built on the instrument we demoted---is the smallest of the
four}, so excluding the adjudicator did not manufacture the result; it removed a
conservative influence on it.

\paragraph{The logit-lens contrasts are not stable.}
That verdict is scoped to R $-$ J, and the scoping is load-bearing. Checking
every contrast against the same two single-judge intervals, \flag{five of nine
are not stable across the two primary judges}. On Qwen, GPT-5 rates J-Lens
\emph{significantly worse} than the logit lens ($-0.680$, \ci{-1.06}{-0.33})
while DeepSeek rates it significantly \emph{better} ($+0.770$,
\ci{0.57}{0.97})---same cells, disjoint intervals, opposite signs. Every
unstable contrast involves the logit lens; every R $-$ J contrast is stable.

One plausible explanation is a floor effect: the judges may agree about the
relative ordering of two Jacobian transports while anchoring the bottom of a
0--4 scale differently when scoring near-noise. We have not tested that
mechanism, and the pattern is also consistent with rubric underdetermination at
the low end. Any comparison against the logit
lens in this report should be read with both judges' values in view and must
not be quoted as a single number.

\paragraph{Agreement.} Quadratic-weighted $\kappa = 0.514$ on 600 paired
scores, exact agreement 39.3\%, mean absolute difference 0.91 points. The two
validated judges disagreed on 63\% of cells---the disagreements the third judge
was introduced to settle, and which the primary rule now simply averages over.
\flag{This is moderate agreement at best.} Validation reduces the concern that this is gross judge failure, but it does not
establish that the disagreement originates in the readouts. It may reflect
genuine ambiguity in early-layer readouts, differences in judge calibration, or
residual underdetermination in the rubric. A $\kappa$ of 0.51 is not a precise
instrument, and every interval in this document inherits that noise.

The effect survives in all 24 subset estimates---every scoring variant, both

\flag{But roughly a third of the pooled gap coincides with an echo difference}
under the equal-echo restriction, and the pattern is not uniform: on Qwen the

irrelevant there.

\paragraph{This is not a causal adjustment.} Echo is measured from the same
readouts as coherence, so restricting on it conditions on a post-treatment
variable. The regression of the coherence difference on the echo difference is
descriptive; its intercept is the fitted difference at equal echo, not an
echo-adjusted effect. \flag{The two judges also disagree about the mechanism}:
GPT-5's slope is $\approx 1.0$--$1.1$ with intervals excluding zero on both
models, while DeepSeek's is $0.29$ \ci{-0.16}{0.80} and $0.35$
\ci{-0.03}{0.63}, both covering zero. GPT-5 sees echo and coherence tightly
coupled; DeepSeek sees almost no coupling. Both still rate R above J.

\paragraph{Read this analysis as a caution, not as reassurance.} Restricting to
cells with equal observed echo selects on a variable that lens identity itself
affects. Conditioning on a post-treatment variable can distort a comparison and
does not estimate the effect with echo removed. The following section measures
the quantity directly, and gives a substantially less reassuring answer than
this restriction does --- which is the point of reporting both.

motivated after seeing the prompt-echo result, its rubric was frozen before any

failure is informative, but no claim in \S\ref{sec:claims} rests on its
estimates.

\flag{The non-echo instrument is markedly less reliable than the standard one.}
Quadratic-weighted $\kappa = 0.19$ against $0.51$ for the standard rubric, exact
agreement 25.8\% against 39.3\%, mean absolute difference 1.44 points against
0.91 on the same 0--4 scale. A $\kappa$ of 0.19 is slight agreement. Every
non-echo number in this section inherits that, and we did not anticipate it: the
copy control established that judges reject a pure copy, which turns out to be a
much easier task than scoring what remains after partial copying.

\flag{The non-echo intervals understate their own uncertainty.} The bootstrap
resamples \emph{prompts}, which is correct for the estimand, but the score
entering each cell is the mean of two judges whose agreement is slight. The
intervals therefore propagate prompt-level variation and \emph{not} judge
disagreement. The per-judge rows above are the honest expression of that second
source, and they lie much further apart than any single interval implies. We also
computed no agreement statistic for \emph{residual substance} itself; the
$\kappa$ above is for the primary non-echo dimension only.

\paragraph{\flag{Judges report less residual material for R-Lens.}} The
non-echo rubric also asks each judge how much non-copied material was there to
score (\emph{residual substance}, 0--2). R-Lens is rated \emph{lower} than
J-Lens on both models: $-0.330$ \ci{-0.395}{-0.265} on Gemma and $-0.150$
\ci{-0.195}{-0.105} on Qwen, both excluding zero.

This is \emph{judge-reported} evidence consistent with the crowd-out concern in
\S\ref{sec:limits}: the non-echo comparison varies both the apparent quality and
the apparent quantity of remaining content. The attenuation therefore conflates
the coherence of the remaining content with the amount of content the judges
perceive as remaining, and the present design cannot apportion the two. Doing so
would require refilling each list from deeper rankings, which we did not do.

We report residual substance as a rating, not as a measurement. With
$\kappa = 0.19$ on the primary non-echo dimension, and no agreement statistic
computed for residual substance itself, it should not be objectified as a count
of tokens.

\paragraph{What strengthens under non-echo scoring.} R-Lens beats the logit
lens on both models; J-Lens clearly does so on Gemma ($+0.875$, $p<10^{-4}$,
against $+0.430$ under standard scoring) and suggestively on Qwen ($+0.250$,
\ci{0.025}{0.465}, $p=0.070$ --- the interval excludes zero but the
pre-specified permutation test does not reject).
J-Lens echoes less than R-Lens, so standard scoring was penalising it for
content the non-echo rubric ignores.

We stop short of concluding that the strongest evidence is about Jacobian
transport in general. Across both rubrics: R $-$ logit is strong on both models;
J $-$ logit is strong on Gemma under non-echo scoring, inconclusive on Qwen
under non-echo scoring ($p=0.070$), and non-significant on both models under
standard scoring, where it also reverses in sign between judges.
\flag{The data do not support a uniform cross-model attribution to the Jacobian
transport alone.} What they support is narrower: on Gemma both Jacobian methods
beat the logit lens on non-copied content, and on Qwen only R-Lens clearly does.

\paragraph{Restriction versus rubric-based exclusion.} These are two different
repairs for the same confound, and neither works cleanly. Restriction
(\S\ref{sec:echo}) retained 74\% of the effect on Qwen and 102\% on Gemma;
rubric-based exclusion retains 47\% and 11\%. Conditioning on equal observed echo and excluding echoed
content answer different questions: the former selects on a lens-affected
variable, the latter changes the scoring construct. Neither identifies a formal
mediation fraction.

Restriction conditions on a post-treatment variable and so cannot estimate a
direct effect. Rubric-based exclusion avoids that specific error but introduces
two of its own: rater instability (\S\ref{sec:mixture}) and confounding of
content quality with content quantity. \flag{Neither method cleanly identifies
the contribution of prompt-local content}, and the sharper methodological result
is that both plausible repairs fail in different ways --- which is more useful to
a reader than a claim that one of them worked.

\subsection{Mixture invariance: the control the rubric failed}
\label{sec:mixture}

The rubric instructs judges to \emph{discard} prompt-local tokens. If they do,
adding more of them to a readout whose meaningful content is unchanged must not
move the score. That is a testable property, and it is the property the panel
actually depends on.

We built 20 families from arms that had already scored $\ge 2$ on this rubric,
so every family had something to lose. Within a family, 0, 3 or 6 positions of a
ten-token list were replaced by prompt-local distractors; every list contained
exactly ten tokens, so adding a distractor \emph{displaced} one core token. Three
distractor kinds were tested separately: literal repetition, case variants, and
near-miss spellings of prompt words. Acceptance criteria were frozen in code
before any rating: median within-family spread $\le 1.0$ on the 0--4 scale, and
at most 34\% of families declining monotonically as padding increases. That
displacement is the design flaw discussed below.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Judge & Median within-family spread & Monotone decline & Verdict \\
\midrule
GPT-5 & $1.00$ (max $1.00$) & 2/20 = 10\% (max 34\%) & PASS \\
DeepSeek-v3.1 & \flag{$2.00$} & \flag{10/20 = 50\%} & \flag{FAIL} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{\flag{This control is confounded by construction, and we report it as
uninformative.}} The specification above is impossible: a candidate list cannot
simultaneously hold the core fixed at ten tokens, hold total length at ten, and
add padding. Our implementation resolved this by letting padding \emph{displace}
core tokens, so the arms carry 10, 7 and 4 core tokens respectively.

That breaks the test. After the judge discards prompt-local tokens, the 6-pad arm
genuinely has less coherent material left --- four tokens rather than ten --- so
a judge scoring it lower may be \emph{correct} rather than padding-sensitive.
\flag{DeepSeek's monotone decline is therefore consistent with correct behaviour,
and GPT-5's flatness is as easily read as the anomaly.} The control cannot
distinguish the two, and we do not use it as evidence about either judge.

We report it because the design error is instructive. Any mixture-invariance test
must sacrifice one of the three constraints: hold the core fixed and let list
length grow (confounding length with padding), hold length fixed and let the core
shrink (what we did, confounding content volume with padding), or vary padding
identity at fixed count (testing something narrower). We specified all three and
did not notice the contradiction until after the ratings were collected.

\paragraph{What still stands without it.} The non-echo instrument's problems do
not depend on this control. Inter-judge agreement is $\kappa = 0.19$ against
$0.51$ for the standard rubric, and on Gemma the two judges produce
opposite-signed estimates with disjoint intervals. Those two facts are measured
directly on the panel itself and are sufficient for the conclusion below.

\paragraph{An earlier version of this control was weaker than it looked.} Our
first attempt did not require the core to be coherent, and drew it from whichever
arm the panel happened to list first --- newline tokens, for poetry cells. Both
judges ``passed'' it, but DeepSeek scored 11 of 20 families at zero throughout,
where the spread is zero however the judge behaves. Restricting that run to the
three families where anything scored above 1 already showed a median spread of
2.00 and 2/3 declining monotonically. The corrected control above reproduces that
at $n = 20$. We report the first attempt because its failure mode --- a control
that passes by measuring nothing --- is worth recording.

\paragraph{What this costs the section.} With $\kappa = 0.19$, a sign
disagreement on Gemma and one judge failing mixture invariance, the non-echo
estimates cannot support a claim about how much of R-Lens's advantage survives
the exclusion of prompt-local content. \flag{What they support is the negative
result: explicitly excluding prompt-local content did not produce a measure two
validated judges agree on.} The decomposition is measurement-sensitive, and that
is worth knowing before anyone builds on it.

score on both models (1.04 against 1.25 on Qwen, 0.71 against 1.13 on Gemma), so
this is not the general pattern --- but we observed one such case and did not
estimate its frequency among punctuation-dominated readouts.

genre, is responsible.

Second, \flag{the prompt-level sign test does not confirm the Qwen non-echo
result} ($p=0.064$, 14 of 19 non-tied prompts positive), even though the primary
estimate excludes zero ($+0.325$, \ci{0.165}{0.480}, $p=0.0042$). The two are
not in contradiction --- the sign test discards magnitude and is the weaker
instrument --- but the Qwen non-echo advantage rests more on the size of the
differences than on a clear majority of prompts, and should be described that
way.

Taken together: the standard-rubric ordering is robust, and the pooled non-echo
estimates are positive but fragile. On Gemma that positive pooled estimate masks
opposite-signed judge estimates; on Qwen both judges are positive but only GPT-5
reaches $p<0.05$. The non-echo findings are therefore not directionally
consistent across judges, and the fragility is compounded rather than merely
observed.

\section{The earlier no-prompt panel}

An earlier 50-cell panel on Qwen returned a null for R $-$ J ($+0.080$, 95\% CI
\ci{-0.16}{0.34}, $p = 0.596$). It is reported here for completeness and is
\textbf{not comparable} to the present result. The two panels differ
simultaneously in prompt visibility (that panel did not show raters the prompt),
size (50 vs 200 cells), rubric scale, judge count, sampling rule and
adjudication, and one of its raters was contaminated. No single one of those
differences can be identified as the reason the results differ, and we do not
claim to know which mattered. It is a preliminary attempt that was superseded,
not a contradicting measurement.

That panel did produce one durable negative result, obtained against 150 human
judgements: of three cheap token-form proxies for coherence, only the
``trash-rate'' proxy correlated in the expected direction ($\rho = -0.640$).
Zero-corpus-frequency rate ($\rho = +0.330$) and in-prompt rate
($\rho = -0.245$) were both \emph{inverted}, retracting two earlier readings of
ours. Token-form statistics are not a substitute for rating the readouts.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{One judge was demoted, not merely unused.} The adjudicator
      cleared 8 of 9 validation gates but its order-invariance rate
      (18.9\%, \ci{9.4\%}{32.0\%}) could neither be admitted nor rejected by a
      feasible battery. We excluded it. A reader who would have admitted it
      should read the adjudicated row of Figure~\ref{fig:judge} as the headline
      instead; it is smaller ($+0.778$ pooled) and still excludes zero.
\item \textbf{Order invariance is weakly controlled for every judge.} The same
      battery gives GPT-5 an interval of \ci{0.2\%}{32\%}. The control is too
      blunt at this size to certify any of the three judges tightly, and a
      larger one was judged not worth its cost.
\item \textbf{Released lenses use the 25-prompt light recipe}, not the
      1000-prompt recipe of the source post. Effect sizes are not comparable to
      the authors'. R-Lens and J-Lens may also differ in sample efficiency, so
      both artifacts use the same fitting-sample size, which removes one source of
      asymmetry, though we did not verify that the fitting prompts or procedures
      were otherwise matched
      without making it equivalent to the full-recipe comparison.
\item \textbf{Model choice was fixed by the capstone brief}: Qwen3.5-27B and
      Gemma-3-27B-it. We did not verify that these are the exact revisions used
      in the source post, and make no claim that they are. All results are
      reported for these artifacts.
\item \textbf{Twenty prompts per model.} Adequate for the pooled estimand,
      which is why intervals are prompt-clustered; not adequate for the
      per-set breakdown, which is descriptive only.
\item \textbf{Autoraters, not humans.} No human ratings were used in the
      primary experiment. A superseded exploratory panel collected 150 human
      judgements and is reported in \S8 only as historical context for a
      negative result about token-form proxies; none of it enters any estimate
      here. Judges were validated against controls, but LLM raters may share
      biases with each other and with the models being read, and no external
      human anchor establishes that their ``contextual coherence'' matches the
      construct a human interpretability researcher cares about.
\item \textbf{Echo is not causally controlled}, only restricted on.
\item \textbf{Logit-lens comparisons are judge-dependent} and should not be
      quoted as single numbers.
\item \textbf{Depth-localisation does not replicate across models.}
\item \textbf{Ordering, not quality.} Nothing here shows early-layer R-Lens
      readouts are coherent in absolute terms; they average roughly 2 of 4, and
      under non-echo scoring less than that.
\item \textbf{The non-echo rubric penalises crowd-out as well as copying.}
      \label{sec:limits}
      Copied tokens both earn no credit \emph{and} occupy slots in a
      fixed-length top-10 list that novel tokens could have filled. Separating
      those would require refilling each list from deeper rankings; the frozen
      panel stored only the top 10, so we did not. Crowd-out may cause this
      analysis to overstate the loss of novel content, but adding lower-ranked
      tokens can move an autorater's score non-monotonically, so the attenuation
      is not a formal bound.
\item \textbf{Four prompts per evaluation set.} Per-set results are descriptive
      and none survives Holm correction.
\item \textbf{One rubric, one panel per construct.} We did not test whether a
      differently-worded non-echo rubric gives the same attenuation.
\item \textbf{Poetry is influential on Gemma.} It is the largest per-set
      difference on both models and the only set read at a different position.
      Removing it takes the Gemma non-echo estimate negative. Four prompts is
      too few to say whether the position, the genre, or chance is responsible.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We evaluate R-Lens's early-layer contextual-coherence claim on Qwen3.5-27B and
Gemma-3-27B-it using a blinded, pre-specified and frozen 200-cell panel scored by
two independently validated LLM autoraters. Under a standard prompt-aware rubric
the claimed advantage reproduces on both models and is robust: under that rubric
the R $-$ J direction survives every judge-aggregation rule we examined, the
deletion of any single prompt, and the deletion of any single evaluation set.

R-Lens, however, also surfaces significantly more prompt-overlapping content.
Under a narrower rubric that discards prompt-local tokens, the R $-$ J advantage
falls by roughly half on Qwen and becomes inconclusive on Gemma.

That second instrument did not validate. Its inter-judge agreement is slight
($\kappa = 0.19$ against $0.51$); on Gemma its two judges disagree in \emph{sign}
with disjoint intervals; one of the two failed a mixture-invariance control,
its scores declining monotonically as ignorable padding was added to a fixed
core; and R-Lens is rated as leaving less material to score in the first place.
We therefore report it as an exploratory measurement study whose result is
negative: \flag{explicitly excluding prompt-local content did not produce a
measure the two judges --- both admitted under the standard protocol, neither
validated for this construct --- agree on.}

The conclusion is neither a clean confirmation nor a refutation. R-Lens's
advantage under ordinary coherence scoring is real and reproducible, but ordinary
coherence conflates novel content with \emph{prompt-local} content --- literal
repetition and normalised reconstruction alike, and the latter may be genuine
computation rather than copying.

We read this as a finding about the construct rather than about the method. A
one-dimensional coherence score silently chooses between prompt-local
reconstruction and abstract contextual representation, and \S\ref{sec:examples}
shows a single cell where that choice reverses which lens wins. Neither choice is
obviously wrong; the source post treats current-token representation as evidence
of early-layer structure, and on that framing R-Lens's behaviour is a success.
What we can say is that ``coherence'', measured one-dimensionally, does not
separate these, and that the ranking of these lenses is not invariant to which
one you mean.

\section{Supported and unsupported claims}
\label{sec:claims}

\paragraph{Supported by these data.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item Under the standard contextual rubric, R-Lens receives higher coherence
      scores than J-Lens on both models, and the direction holds for each
      validated judge separately.
\item R-Lens surfaces significantly more prompt-overlapping content than J-Lens
      on both models.
\item The R $-$ J gap is substantially smaller under a rubric that excludes
      prompt-overlapping content.
\item Descriptively, the pooled R $-$ J difference was smaller under the
      exploratory non-echo rubric. Because that rubric achieved only slight
      inter-judge agreement ($\kappa = 0.19$) and produced opposite-signed
      estimates on Gemma, we do \emph{not} interpret the attenuation as evidence
      about relative lens quality.
\item Excluding prompt-local content did not produce a measure the two judges
      agree on. This is a negative result about the construct; no lens
      comparison in this list rests on non-echo estimates.
\item Under the standard rubric, R $-$ logit is positive and significant on
      both models. The corresponding non-echo estimates are reported in
      \S\ref{sec:nonecho} as exploratory and are not offered as inferential
      conclusions.
\item The standard-rubric R $-$ J difference survives deletion of any single
      prompt and any single evaluation set, on both models
      (\S\ref{sec:smallsample}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{NOT supported, and not claimed.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item That R-Lens is universally more semantically informative than J-Lens.
\item That a specific percentage of the standard R $-$ J effect \emph{is caused
      by} prompt echo. The attenuations are construct changes, not mediation
      fractions.
\item That restricting to equal observed echo identifies the direct effect of
      lens type. It conditions on a lens-affected variable.
\item That the source post's $n{=}1000$ fitting recipe was replicated. We
      evaluate the released $n{=}25$ light-recipe artifacts.
\item That autorater scores equal human judgement, or that the rubric is a
      validated measure of interpretation quality. The control battery shows the
      judges follow the intended behaviours; it does not validate the construct.
\item That J-Lens consistently beats the logit lens under the standard rubric.
      That contrast is judge-dependent and reverses in sign between judges.
\item That the excluded adjudicator was shown to be order-biased.
\item That a non-significant result establishes equality.
\item That five depths per prompt constitute independent observations. The
      inferential unit is the prompt: 20 per model.
\item That the non-echo findings are as stable as the standard ones. The
      non-echo rubric has $\kappa = 0.19$; on Gemma the two judges disagree in
      sign; the Gemma estimate turns negative when the poetry set is removed;
      and neither model's non-echo effect is confirmed by a prompt-level sign
      test at $\alpha = 0.05$.
\item That the Gemma non-echo null demonstrates the absence of an advantage. It
      reflects disagreement between two judges, not agreement that there is
      nothing there.
\item That the non-echo attenuation measures loss of novel content alone. R-Lens
      also has less residual material to be scored, and the two are not
      separable without refilling from deeper rankings.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What would change the conclusion}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item A human panel of even modest size disagreeing with the autoraters on the
      standard rubric would undercut every coherence claim here at once, since
      nothing in this document is anchored to human judgement.
\item A refilled non-echo analysis --- filtering prompt copies from a deeper
      ranking and topping the list back up to ten --- would separate loss of
      novel content from top-10 crowd-out. If the Gemma null persisted after
      refilling, the reading would firm up to ``R-Lens has no detectable
      advantage in novel content on Gemma''. If it became positive, the
      attenuation would be largely a presentation effect.
\item Reviving comparative non-echo claims would require a redesigned
      instrument: one achieving acceptable inter-rater agreement, and a
      mixture-invariance test whose three constraints are not mutually
      contradictory (\S\ref{sec:mixture}), passing for both judges.
\item Refitting both lenses at $n = 1000$ and finding the gap disappears would
      indicate the effect is a property of the light recipe.
\end{itemize}

\section{The two rubrics, in full}
\label{sec:rubrics}

Hashes establish immutability, not validity. Both system prompts are reproduced
verbatim so a reader can judge what the raters were actually asked. Each was
presented as the system message; the user message carried the prompt with the
readout position marked \texttt{[[>>token<<]]}, the position index, and the three
candidate arms as ranked token lists labelled A, B and C.

\subsection*{Standard rubric (salt \texttt{coherence-v2-2026-08-26})}
\begin{quote}\ttfamily\scriptsize\raggedright
You are rating three anonymous readouts of the SAME internal state of a
language model, taken at the SAME highlighted position in the prompt below. Each
candidate was produced by a different decoding method. You do not know which is which,
and you must not speculate about their identity.

Treat each readout as a BAG OF TOKEN-LEVEL CONCEPTS, not as a grammatical sentence.
Token fragments may jointly express a meaningful word. Do NOT penalise valid
non-English words, transliterations, or non-Latin scripts merely for not being English.
Whitespace or punctuation may be contextually appropriate — judge it in context.

Score each candidate on three scales.

CONTEXTUAL COHERENCE (0-4) — the primary measure. Does the readout form a meaningful
interpretation of the model's state at the highlighted position?
  0: Predominantly malformed, uninterpretable, or semantically unrelated.
  1: Mostly incoherent; at most one weak or plausibly accidental contextual connection.
  2: Partially coherent; at least one clear relevant concept or theme, but substantial
     noise or ambiguity remains.
  3: Mostly coherent; multiple tokens support a meaningful theme related to the prompt
     at the highlighted position.
  4: Strongly coherent; a clear, semantically unified, contextually appropriate
     representation.
Prompt copying alone CAN be coherent, but must not automatically earn a high score.
Score whether the readout forms a meaningful contextual representation.

LEXICAL INTEGRITY (0-2) — are the outputs interpretable units, regardless of relevance?
  0: Mostly malformed fragments, special tokens, punctuation runs, or meaningless
     repetitions.
  1: A mixture of interpretable lexical items and malformed/noisy items.
  2: Mostly recognisable words or meaningful token fragments.

PROMPT ECHO (0-2) — how much is direct repetition of visible input? Neither good nor bad.
  0: Little or no direct copying of nearby prompt tokens.
  1: Some copying, alongside additional content.
  2: Dominated by direct prompt copying or trivial lexical variants.

Also return the contextual winner: "A", "B", "C", or "tie", and for each candidate an
evidence statement of at most 25 words. Do not emit hidden reasoning.

Return STRICT JSON only, exactly this shape:
\{"A": \{"contextual\_coherence": 0, "lexical\_integrity": 0, "prompt\_echo": 0, "evidence": ""\},
 "B": \{"contextual\_coherence": 0, "lexical\_integrity": 0, "prompt\_echo": 0, "evidence": ""\},
 "C": \{"contextual\_coherence": 0, "lexical\_integrity": 0, "prompt\_echo": 0, "evidence": ""\},
 "contextual\_winner": "A|B|C|tie"\}
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Non-echo rubric (salt \texttt{non-echo-2026-08-27})}
\begin{quote}\ttfamily\scriptsize\raggedright
You are rating three anonymous readouts of the SAME internal state of a
language model, taken at the SAME highlighted position in the prompt below. Each
candidate was produced by a different decoding method. You do not know which is which,
and you must not speculate about their identity.

Treat each readout as a BAG OF TOKEN-LEVEL CONCEPTS, not as a grammatical sentence.
Token fragments may jointly express a meaningful word. Do NOT penalise valid
non-English words, transliterations, or non-Latin scripts merely for not being English.

THE CENTRAL INSTRUCTION. Some readouts repeat words that already appear in the prompt.
Before scoring, mentally DISCARD every token that merely repeats a word visible in the
prompt above, including trivial variants (case, leading space, plural, a shared word
stem). Score only what REMAINS after discarding those. A readout that is entirely
prompt repetition has nothing remaining and scores 0, however fluent it looks.

NON-ECHO COHERENCE (0-4) — the primary measure. Of the tokens that are NOT copied from
the prompt, do they form a meaningful interpretation of the model's state at the
highlighted position?
  0: Nothing remains after discarding copied tokens, or what remains is malformed,
     uninterpretable, or semantically unrelated.
  1: What remains is mostly incoherent; at most one weak or plausibly accidental
     contextual connection.
  2: What remains is partially coherent; at least one clear relevant concept or theme,
     but substantial noise or ambiguity.
  3: What remains is mostly coherent; multiple non-copied tokens support a meaningful
     theme related to the prompt at the highlighted position.
  4: What remains is strongly coherent; a clear, semantically unified, contextually
     appropriate representation built from non-copied tokens.

RESIDUAL SUBSTANCE (0-2) — how much non-copied material was there to judge?
  0: Essentially nothing; the readout is dominated by prompt repetition.
  1: Some non-copied material alongside substantial repetition.
  2: Mostly non-copied material.
Report this honestly. It is a description of the readout, not a quality score, and a
low value does NOT justify raising or lowering non-echo coherence.

Also return the non-echo winner: "A", "B", "C", or "tie", and for each candidate an
evidence statement of at most 25 words naming which tokens you discarded as copied.
Do not emit hidden reasoning.

Return STRICT JSON only, exactly this shape:
\{"A": \{"non\_echo\_coherence": 0, "residual\_substance": 0, "evidence": ""\},
 "B": \{"non\_echo\_coherence": 0, "residual\_substance": 0, "evidence": ""\},
 "C": \{"non\_echo\_coherence": 0, "residual\_substance": 0, "evidence": ""\},
 "non\_echo\_winner": "A|B|C|tie"\}
\end{quote}
